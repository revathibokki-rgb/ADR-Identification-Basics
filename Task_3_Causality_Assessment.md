# Task 3 – Causality Assessment

## Objective
To evaluate the relationship between a suspected drug and an adverse drug reaction (ADR) using a standard qualitative causality assessment approach and classify the reaction as **Certain, Probable, or Possible**.

## Method
The cases were assessed using the key principles of the **WHO-UMC qualitative causality assessment system**:

- **Certain:** A reasonable temporal relationship exists; the reaction cannot reasonably be explained by the disease or other drugs; the clinical event is consistent with the suspected drug; and withdrawal/dechallenge provides appropriate clinical evidence.
- **Probable/Likely:** A reasonable temporal relationship exists; other causes are unlikely; the response to withdrawal is clinically reasonable; rechallenge is not required.
- **Possible:** A reasonable temporal relationship exists, but the reaction could also reasonably be explained by the underlying disease or another drug.

> Rechallenge should not be performed solely for causality assessment when it may expose a patient to significant risk.

---

## Case 1 – Metoclopramide-Induced Acute Dystonia

**Patient:** 22-year-old female  
**Suspected drug:** Metoclopramide 10 mg orally  
**Indication:** Nausea and vomiting  
**ADR:** Acute dystonic reaction with neck stiffness, jaw spasm and abnormal eye deviation

### Clinical course
The patient developed involuntary neck and facial muscle contractions approximately 4 hours after taking metoclopramide. She had no previous neurological disorder and was not taking other medicines known to cause dystonia. Metoclopramide was discontinued and the symptoms resolved rapidly after treatment with an anticholinergic agent.

### Causality assessment
- **Temporal relationship:** Strong and clinically plausible.
- **Known association:** Acute dystonia is a recognised adverse effect of dopamine D2-receptor antagonists such as metoclopramide.
- **Alternative explanations:** No relevant underlying neurological condition or interacting drug was identified.
- **Dechallenge:** Symptoms resolved after withdrawal and appropriate treatment.
- **Rechallenge:** Not performed because intentional re-exposure would be inappropriate.

### Classification: **CERTAIN**

**Reason:** The timing, known pharmacological effect, absence of a reasonable alternative explanation and clear improvement after withdrawal strongly support a causal relationship.

## Case 2 – Enalapril-Induced Persistent Dry Cough

**Patient:** 48-year-old male  
**Suspected drug:** Enalapril 10 mg once daily  
**Indication:** Hypertension  
**ADR:** Persistent dry cough

### Clinical course
The patient developed a persistent non-productive cough approximately 3 weeks after starting enalapril. There was no fever, respiratory infection, smoking history or evidence of pulmonary disease. Other medicines remained unchanged. Enalapril was discontinued and the cough gradually resolved over the following weeks.

### Causality assessment
- **Temporal relationship:** Compatible with the known onset of ACE-inhibitor cough.
- **Known association:** Dry cough is a well-established adverse effect of ACE inhibitors.
- **Alternative explanations:** No convincing alternative cause was identified.
- **Dechallenge:** Improvement occurred after discontinuation.
- **Rechallenge:** Not required.

### Classification: **PROBABLE**

**Reason:** The temporal relationship and positive dechallenge support the association, while the absence of alternative causes makes another explanation unlikely. Rechallenge is unnecessary because ACE-inhibitor cough is well recognised and re-exposure is not clinically required.

## Case 3 – Amoxicillin-Associated Skin Rash

**Patient:** 19-year-old female  
**Suspected drug:** Amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily  
**Indication:** Bacterial respiratory infection  
**ADR:** Generalised maculopapular rash

### Clinical course
A generalised non-severe maculopapular rash developed 2 days after starting amoxicillin. The patient was also experiencing an acute viral-like respiratory illness, and no diagnostic testing was available to establish whether the illness itself contributed to the rash. Amoxicillin was stopped and the rash gradually improved.

### Causality assessment
- **Temporal relationship:** Compatible with a drug-related reaction.
- **Known association:** Cutaneous reactions are recognised with amoxicillin.
- **Alternative explanations:** The underlying infectious illness could also contribute to the rash.
- **Dechallenge:** Improvement occurred after withdrawal, but spontaneous improvement of an infection-related rash is also possible.
- **Rechallenge:** Not performed.

### Classification: **POSSIBLE**

**Reason:** Amoxicillin is a plausible cause, but the underlying illness provides a reasonable alternative explanation. Therefore, the evidence is insufficient to classify the reaction as probable or certain.

## Case 4 – Atorvastatin-Associated Myalgia

**Patient:** 56-year-old male  
**Suspected drug:** Atorvastatin 40 mg once daily  
**Indication:** Hyperlipidaemia  
**ADR:** Bilateral muscle aches and tenderness

### Clinical course
The patient developed bilateral thigh and calf muscle pain approximately 4 weeks after atorvastatin was initiated. No strenuous exercise or trauma was reported. Other medicines were unchanged. Creatine kinase was not markedly elevated. Atorvastatin was discontinued, after which the symptoms improved substantially. No alternative cause was identified.

### Causality assessment
- **Temporal relationship:** Compatible with statin-associated muscle symptoms.
- **Known association:** Myalgia is a recognised adverse effect of statins.
- **Alternative explanations:** No convincing alternative cause was identified.
- **Dechallenge:** Symptoms improved following withdrawal.
- **Rechallenge:** Not required.

### Classification: **PROBABLE**

**Reason:** The timing, recognised adverse-effect profile, absence of another clear cause and positive dechallenge support a probable relationship.

## Case 5 – Omeprazole and Headache

**Patient:** 35-year-old female  
**Suspected drug:** Omeprazole 20 mg once daily  
**Indication:** Dyspepsia  
**ADR:** Headache

### Clinical course
The patient developed a mild intermittent headache 3 days after starting omeprazole. She was also under significant work-related stress and reported reduced sleep during the same period. No neurological abnormalities were identified. Omeprazole was continued and the headache resolved intermittently without specific treatment.

### Causality assessment
- **Temporal relationship:** Possible.
- **Known association:** Headache has been reported with omeprazole.
- **Alternative explanations:** Sleep deprivation and stress provide plausible alternative causes.
- **Dechallenge:** No meaningful dechallenge was observed because the medicine was continued.
- **Rechallenge:** Not performed.

### Classification: **POSSIBLE**

**Reason:** The timing is compatible with an ADR, but significant alternative explanations are present and there is no supportive dechallenge evidence.

## Conclusion
Causality assessment helps determine how strongly an ADR can be attributed to a suspected medicine. In these cases, the classification depended mainly on the temporal relationship, known pharmacological association, presence or absence of alternative explanations, and response after drug withdrawal. The examples demonstrate that a positive dechallenge can strengthen causality, while a plausible alternative explanation may reduce the classification to possible.
